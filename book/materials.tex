\subsection{Rivlin}

Let $I_1, I_2, I_3$ be the three invariants of the two Cauchy-Green 
deformation tensors.  The invariants are defined in terms of principal
stretch ratios $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$,
\begin{align}
I_1 & = \lambda_1^2 + \lambda_2^2 + \lambda_3^2, \\
I_2 & = \lambda_1^2 \lambda_2^2 + \lambda_2^2 \lambda_3^2 + \lambda_3^2 \lambda_1^2, \\
I_3 & = \lambda_1^2 \lambda_2^2 \lambda_3^2 = J^2.
\end{align}
Rivlin\footnote{Rivlin RS. Solution of Some Problems in the Exact Theory
of Visco-Elasticity, J Rational Mechanics and Analysis, 5:1 (1956), 179-188.}
specified a strain energy density function $W$ as 
power series of the invariants,
\be
 W = \sum_{i+j+k=1}^{\infty} C_{ijk} (I_1 - 3)^i \; (I_2 - 3)^j \; (I_3 - 1)^k.
\ee
For {\bf incompressible} materials, $I_3 = 1$, and 
the Rivlin equation reduces to
\be
 W = \sum_{i+j=1}^{\infty} C_{ij} (I_1 - 3)^i \; (I_2 - 3)^j.
\ee

\begin{Hremark}
The presence of the $-3, -3$, and $-1$ terms in parenthesis allow for
zero strain energy density when there is zero stretch.
\end{Hremark}

\begin{Hremark}
When a material's bulk modulus $B$ is at least two orders of magnitude 
larger than the shear modulus $G$, 
the incompressibility modeling assumption may be appropriate.
\end{Hremark}

\subsubsection{Incompressible Rivlin}
Using on the first two terms of the incompressible Rivlin gives 
the {\bf Mooney-Rivlin} strain energy density function:
\be
 W = C_{10} (I_1 - 3) + C_{01} (I_2 - 3).
\ee

\subsubsection{Incompressible Neo-Hookean}
Using only the first term of the Mooney-Rivlin equation gives the 
{\bf Incompressible Neo-Hookean} strain energy density function:
\be
 W = C_{10} (I_1 - 3).
\ee
The invariants in terms of the right Cauchy-Green tensor $\tCbold$ 
and left Cauchy-Green tensor $\tbbold$ are
\begin{align}
    I_1 & = \trace(\tCbold) = C_{KK} =
            \trace(\tbbold) = b_{kk}, \\
	I_2 & = \half \left(I_1^2 - \tCbold \ddiv \tCbold \right) = 
	        \half \left(I_1^2 - C_{IK} C_{KI} \right) \nonumber \\ 
	    & = \half \left(I_1^2 - \tbbold \ddiv \tbbold \right) = 
	        \half \left(I_1^2 - b_{ik} b_{ki} \right), \\
	I_3 & = \det(\tCbold) = \det C_{IJ} = 
            \det(\tbbold) = \det b_{ij} = J^2.
\end{align}
For small strains and incompressible Mooney-Rivlin:
\begin{align}
	E & = 6 \left(C_{10} + C_{01} \right), \\
	G & = 2 \left(C_{10} + C_{01} \right).
\end{align}
For small strains and incompressible Neo-Hookean:
\begin{align}
	C_{01} & = 0, \\
	C_{10} & = G/2, \implies E = 3 G. 
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Compressible Rivlin}
Next, allow the material to be compressible, characterized through 
bulk modulus $B$.  Thus, $I_3 \neq 1$.
The {\bf Mooney-Rivlin equation} for compressible materials is given by
\begin{align}
 W & = W_{\smVOL}(I_3) +  C_{10} (I_1 - 3) + C_{01} (I_2 - 3),  \\
   & = W_{\smVOL}(I_3) +  C_{10} (I_1 - 3) + C_{01} (I_2 - 3),  
\end{align}
where $W_{\smVOL}$ is the hydrostatic (volumetric) term.  

To fully uncouple the volumetric from the deviatoric responses, let the
{\bf volume preserving (isochoric) distortion invariants} be calculated
as
\begin{align}
    \bar{I}_1 & = \frac{I_1}{I_3^{(1/3)}} = \frac{I_1}{J^{(2/3)}}, \\
    \bar{I}_2 & = \frac{I_2}{I_3^{(2/3)}} = \frac{I_1}{J^{(4/3)}}, \\
	J & = \sqrt{I_3}.
\end{align}
Then, 
for {\bf compressible Rivlin}:
\be
 W = \underset{\smVOL}{\underbrace{\frac{B}{2}(J - 1)^2}} + 
 \underset{\smDEV}{\underbrace{C_{10} (\bar{I}_1 - 3) + C_{01} (\bar{I}_2 - 3)}}.
\ee
Finally, 
for {\bf compressible Neo-Hookean}:
\be
 W = \underset{\smVOL}{\underbrace{\frac{B}{2}(J - 1)^2}} + 
 \underset{\smDEV}{\underbrace{C_{10} (\bar{I}_1 - 3)}}. 
\ee

In the finite compression limit, as the volume goes to zero
$(J \rightarrow 0)$, 
the $(J-1)^2$ factor in the volumetric term tends toward unity.
This behavior---finite 
strain energy density in the limit of compression---is not desirable.  
Instead, as the volume goes toward zero, 
the strain energy density so go toward
$+\infty$.    Simo and 
Hughes\footnote{Simo JC, Hughes TJR.  Computational inelasticity (Vol.~7). 
Springer Science \& Business Media, 2006, page 361, Eq.~(10.4.19).} 
provided an alternative form of the 
compressible Neo-Hookean strain energy density that exhibits this desired 
response,
\be
 W = \underset{\smVOL}{
        \underbrace{
            \frac{B}{2}
            \left[
                \frac{J^2 - 1}{2} - \ln J
            \right]
        }
     } 
     + 
 \underset{\smDEV}{
        \underbrace{
            \frac{G}{2} (\bar{I}_1 - 3)
        }
    }. 
    \label{eq:Hughes_neo_hookean}
\ee
Figure~\ref{fig:neo_hookean_vol}
shows the volumetric term for the infinitesimal and finite cases, with the
relative contribution of the two terms that add to compose the finite case.

\begin{Hremark}
The Simo and Hughes notation, while slightly different from above, is equivalent.
    For the deviatoric term, since 
\be
    \bar{\tFbold} = \frac{\tFbold}{J^{(1/3)}},
\ee
    and
\be
    \bar{\tCbold} = \bar{\tFbold}^T \bar{\tFbold} = 
    \frac{\tFbold^T}{J^{(1/3)}} \frac{\tFbold}{J^{(1/3)}} 
    = \frac{\tFbold^T \tFbold}{J^{(2/3)}}
    = \frac{\tCbold}{J^{(2/3)}},
\ee
then,
\be
 \trace(\bar{\tCbold}) = 
    \trace\left(\frac{\tCbold}{J^{(2/3)}}\right) = 
    J^{(-2/3)} \trace(\tCbold) = \frac{I_1}{J^{(2/3)}} = \bar{I_1},
\ee
which harmonizes the notation used here with the deviatoric term from Simo and
Hughes.

\begin{Hremark}
    Consider the relative effects of the $(J^2 - 1)/2$ term versus the 
    $-\ln(J)$ term in 
    (\ref{eq:Hughes_neo_hookean}):  
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For finite compression, the $-\ln(J)$ dominates the $(J^2-1)/2$ term, 
            causing the strain energy function to go toward $+\infty$, as 
            desired.
        \item For finite extension, the $(J^2-1)/2$ term dominates the $-\ln(J)$
            term, causing the strain energy function to go toward $+\infty$, as
            desired.
        \item The addition of the two terms evaluated at $J=1$ gives 
            $W=0$, as
            desired.  For zero volumetric deformation, there should be zero 
            strain energy density.
        \item The slope of the strain energy density function, $dW/dJ = 
            J - 1/J$, evaluated
            at $J=1$ is zero.  For zero volumetric deformation, there should
            be zero initial stress. 
        \item The second derivative of the strain energy density function, 
            $d^2W/dJ^2 = (1 + 1/J^2)$ is positive for all $J \in (0, +\infty)$.  
            Thus, the volumetric strain energy density function $W_{\smVOL}$ is convex.
    \end{itemize}
\end{Hremark}

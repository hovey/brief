\chapter{Formulation}

\section{Fixed Places}

Let $\mathfrak{X}$ denote a {\bf fixed place}, which will be understood as a fundamental and elementary answer to the question of ``Where?''
For now, a fixed place is understood without recourse to concepts of location or position, since location and position require at least {\em two} fixed places to be defined (the subject fixed place itself and an origin's fixed place).\footnote{With the notions of space coordinates, introduced in the following section, we can think of a fixed place as a location that has an immutable position.}
Let $\mathbb{S}$ denote {\bf space}, defined as the infinite set of fixed places $\mathfrak{X}$.


\section{Coordinates}

Now, we define the concept of location of fixed places through introduction of an origin, bases vectors, which embed both a direction and a magnitude on the space $\mathbb{S}$.  
With space, an origin, and a basis, {\bf coordinates} arise as a measure of the distance from the origin to the fixed place, understood as the location.
Coordinates are measure, given a particular {\bf coordinate system}.  The coordinate system considered herein will be the Cartesian coordinate system, as opposed to the polar coordinate system or the spherical coordinate system.  

\subsection{Space-Fixed Coordinates}

From the infinite set of fixed places $\mathbb{S}$, we select one fixed place to designate as the {\bf origin} $o$.  (See Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial_points}.)

  \label{fig:spatial_points} % label must come after caption, not before!
\end{figure}

The term {\bf dextral} designates a {\em right-handed rule}, 
such that 
$\veone \times \vetwo = \vethree$, 
$\vetwo \times \vethree = \veone$, and
$\vethree \times \veone = \vetwo$, or in a more
compact notation, that
$\vesubi \times \vesubj = \vesubk$.
The term {\bf orthonormal} indicates that the basis vectors are straight and are mutually perpendicular.  
The term {\bf unit} indicates the length of each vector is unity.  Thus,
\be
\norm{\veone} \;\;=\;\;\norm{\vetwo} \;\;=\;\; 
\norm{\vethree} \;\;= 1.
\ee

The origin $o$ and the spatial basis vectors $\veone, \vetwo, \vethree$ together compose the {\bf spatial coordinate system} $S$, which is a one-to-one correspondence between fixed places $\mathfrak{X}$ in space $\mathbb{S}$ and their {\bf spatial coordinates} $\vx$.
The spatial coordinates are an ordered set of three real numbers $\{x_1, x_2, x_3\}\transpose$ measured in a particular coordinate system from the coordinate system's origin to the fixed place $\mathfrak{X}$ in space $\mathbb{S}$.  

A place $\mathfrak{X}$ in space $\mathbb{S}$ is {\em located} the spatial coordinate system $S$ by the particular spatial coordinate $\vx$, written in {\bf explicit form} as
\be
\vx = x_1 \veone + x_2 \vetwo + x_3 \vethree = x_i \vesubi.
\ee
Spatial coordinates locate the position of a of place
$\mathfrak{X}$ in space $\mathbb{S}$.
Thus the coordinate system $S$ is a map from a place $\mathfrak{X}$, which is a member of space $\mathbb{S}$, to a coordinate in real, three-dimensional space.  Mathematically, we write, for $\mathfrak{X} \in \mathbb{S}$ and 
$\vx \in \realthree$,
\be
 S : \mathfrak{X} \mapsto \vx.
\ee

Since, at the present moment, we have only introduced one coordinate system (and thus only one origin and one set of basis vectors), we need not designate the basis vectors explicitly, and may write in {\bf implicit form},
\be
\vx = \{x_1, x_2, x_3\}\transpose,
\label{eq:no_basis}
\ee 
without ambiguity.
For simplicity, we sometimes will write the vector $\vx$ as a triple, 
\be
\vx = (x_1, x_2, x_3),
\ee
without the braces and the transpose, which are formalisms to explicitly indicate a vector.
This abbreviated form (\ref{eq:no_basis}), however, will cause ambiguity later, when two or more coordinate systems are present to describe places in space.  In this latter case, the explicit basis vectors or the explicit coordinate system must be stated to avoid confusion.  
To explicitly state the coordinate system in which the coordinates are measures, we indicate the basis vector, such as in $x_i \vesubi$.

Next, envision a body as a collection of particles existing in space.  Denote the set of particles as $B \in \realthree$.  
From either one of the particles in body $B$, or from a particles that moves with the body,\footnote{A particle not on the body but moving with the body will be referred to as a {\em carrier frame}, defined later.} we selected one reference point as the {\bf body origin} $O$. (See Fig.~\ref{fig:body_points}.)

At the body origin $O$, we construct three dextral, orthonormal {be unit vectors} $\vEone$, $\vEtwo$, $\vEthree$, fixed in the body, called {\bf material basis vectors}.
The origin $O$ and the material basis vectors $\vEone$, $\vEtwo$, $\vEthree$ together compose the {\bf material coordinate system} $M$, which is a one-to-one correspondence between places in both $B$ and their material coordinates $\vX$.
The material coordinates are an ordered set of three real numbers $\{X_1, X_2, X_3\}\transpose$ measured in the body's coordinate system.  
A particle in body $B$ is {\em located} in the material coordinate system $M$ by the particular material coordinate $\vX$, written in {\bf explicit form} as 
\be
\vX = X_1 \vEone + X_2 \vEtwo + X_3 \vEthree = X_I \vEsubI.
\ee
Thus, material coordinates locate a particle in a body $B$.

If we do not designate the basis vectors explicitly with capital letters,  we may write in {\bf implicit form},
\be
\vX = \{X_1, X_2, X_3\}\transpose,
\label{eq:X_no_basis}
\ee 
without ambiguity as measures with respect to the body coordinate $M$.
For simplicity, we sometimes will write the vector $\vX$ as a triple, 
\be
\vX = (X_1, X_2, X_3),
\ee
without the braces and the transpose, which are formalisms to explicitly indicate a vector.

We have now introduced two coordinate systems: the space-fixed coordinate system and the body-fixed coordinate system.  To speak of a particle measured with space coordinates, we will need a way to transform body coordinates into space coordinates.  This will be achieved through a coordinate transformation.  Two lay the groundwork for this transformation, we first define the concept of the {\bf reference configuration}.

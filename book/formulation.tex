\chapter{Formulation}

\section{Fixed Places}

\begin{itemize}

\item Let $\mathfrak{X}$ denote a {\bf fixed place}, 
which will be understood as a
fundamental and elementary answer to the question of ``Where?''

\item For now, a fixed place is understood without 
recourse to concepts of location or position, which require at least 
{\em two} fixed places to be defined (the subject fixed place itself and 
an origin's fixed place).

\item Let $\mathbb{S}$ denote {\bf space}, 
defined as the infinite set of fixed places
$\mathfrak{X}$.

\item With the notions of space coordinates, introduced in the following 
section, we can further think of a fixed place as a location 
that has an immutable position. 

\end{itemize}

\section{Space Coordinates}
\begin{itemize}
\item From the infinite set of fixed places $\mathbb{S}$, we select
one fixed place to designate as the {\bf origin} $O$.

\item At the origin $O$, we construct three dextral, orthonormal {\bf unit
vectors}, $\veeonehat, \veetwohat, \veethreehat$, called the 
{\bf spatial basis vectors}.

\item The term {\bf dextral} designates a {\em right-handed rule}, 
such that 
$\veeonehat \times \veetwohat = \veethreehat$, 
$\veetwohat \times \veethreehat = \veeonehat$, and
$\veethreehat \times \veeonehat = \veetwohat$, or in a more
compact notation, that
$\veeihat \times \veejhat = \veekhat$.

\item The term {\bf orthonormal} indicates that the basis vectors are
straight and are mutually perpendicular.  

\item The term {\bf unit} indicates the length of each vector is unity.
Thus, 
\be
\norm{\veeonehat} \;\;=\;\;\norm{\veetwohat} \;\;=\;\; 
\norm{\veethreehat} \;\;= 1.
\ee
Whereas the notation $\ve{e}$ denotes a vector of unspecified 
length, the overhat notation
$\hat{\ve{e}}$ denotes a {\em unit} vector.

\item The origin $O$ and the spatial basis vectors $\veeonehat, \veetwohat, 
\veethreehat$ together compose the {\bf spatial coordinate system} $S$, which is
a one-to-one correspondence between fixed places $\mathfrak{X}$
in space $\mathbb{S}$ and their {\bf spatial coordinates} $\vx$.

\item The spatial coordinates are an ordered set of three real numbers
$\{x_1, x_2, x_3\}\transpose$ measured in a particular coordinate system
from the coordinate system's origin to the fixed place $\mathfrak{X}$
in space $\mathbb{S}$.  


\item A place $\mathfrak{X}$ in space $\mathbb{S}$ 
is {\em located} the spatial coordinate system $S$ by the 
particular spatial
coordinate $\vx$, written explicitly as
\be
\vx = x_1 \veeonehat + x_2 \veetwohat + x_3 \veethreehat = x_i \veeihat.
\ee
Thus, spatial coordinates locate the position of a of place
$\mathfrak{X}$ in space $\mathbb{S}$.

\item Thus the coordinate system $S$ is a map from 
a place $\mathfrak{X}$, which is a member of space $\mathbb{S}$, to a 
coordinate in real, three-dimensional space.  
Mathematically, we write, for $\mathfrak{X} \in \mathbb{S}$ and 
$\vx \in \realthree$,
\be
 S : \mathfrak{X} \mapsto \vx.
\ee

\item Since, at the present moment, 
we have only introduced one coordinate system (and thus only
one origin and one set of basis vectors), 
we need not designate the basis vectors explicitly, 
and may simply write 
\be
\vx = \{x_1, x_2, x_3\}\transpose
\label{eq:no_basis}
\ee 
without ambiguity.

\item This abbreviated form (\ref{eq:no_basis}), 
however, will cause ambiguity
later, when two or more coordinate systems are present to describe
places in space.  In this latter case, 
the explicit basis vectors or the explicit coordinate system must be 
stated to avoid confusion.

\end{itemize}

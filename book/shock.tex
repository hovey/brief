\section{Elastodynamics}

The bulk modulus $B$, a measure of local compressibility of a substance, is
defined as the ratio of infinitesimal change in pressure $dP$ caused by an
infinitesimal change in volume $dV$, at a given volume $V$
\be 
B \defe - V \dst\frac{dP}{dV}.
\ee
We require $B > 0$, and since $V > 0$, then $dP/dV < 0$.
This results in behaviors observed physically, namely that, all else being
equal, 
\begin{itemize}
\item increase in pressure causes decrease in volume, and vice versa, 
$\uparrow P \Longleftrightarrow \;\downarrow V$, and
\item increase in volume causes decrease in pressure, and vice versa, 
$\uparrow V \Longleftrightarrow \;\downarrow P$.
\end{itemize}
Using the definition of density $\rho \defe m / V$, 
\be
\frac{d\rho}{dV} = \frac{-m}{V^2},
\ee
and
\be
\frac{dP}{dV} = \frac{dP}{d\rho}\frac{d\rho}{dV},
\ee
the bulk modulus $B$ may also be written as
\be
B = \rho \frac{dP}{d\rho}.
\ee
Since $B > 0$ and $\rho > 0$, then $dP/d\rho > 0$.  This result physically means
that, all else being equal, 
\begin{itemize}
\item increases in pressure cause increases in density (and vice versa), and
\item increases in density cause increases in pressure (and vice versa).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Properties}
Thermodynamics properties can categorized as either {\bf intensive} 
or {\bf extensive}.  
\begin{itemize}
\item Intensive properties are independent of, and therefore not 
proportional to, the size or sample of the material.
Intensive properties are also referred to as {\bf intrinsic} properties
or, equivalently, as {\bf bulk} properties.
\item In contrast, extensive properties are 
dependent on, and therefore proportional
to, the size or sample of the material.  
Extrinsic properties are also referred to as {\bf extrinsic} properties.
\end{itemize}

It is often convenient to speak of extrinsic properties in away that makes
them {\em independent} of size or sample of the material.  
To achieve this, the
extrinsic properties are divided by the mass of the sample. 
This ratio is referred to 
as the specific extrinsic property.  
Two common examples of specific properties
are the {\bf specific volume} and {\bf specific energy}, defined as 
\be
\svol \defe \frac{V}{m} = \frac{1}{\rho},
\label{eq:specific_volume}
\ee
and
\be
\senergy \defe \frac{E}{m}.
\ee


\subsection{First Law of Thermodynamics}
The first law for a closed system states the change in internal
energy $\Delta \IE$ is equal to the net energy $Q$ added to the system as heat
less the net work $W$ done {\bf by} the system.
\be
\Delta \IE = Q - W.
\ee
This was the sign convention used by Joule and Clausius.
A different sign convention, used by Planck, stated that 
the change in internal energy $\Delta \IE$ is equal to the net energy $Q$
added to the system as heat plus the net work done {\bf on} the system.
\be
\Delta \IE = Q + W.
\ee
Thus, all net transfers {\bf to} the system are {\bf positive}.  In 
contrast, all net transfers {\bf from} the system are {\bf negative}.

Then, the conservation of energy principle for open systems can be
written as
\be
\Delta E = \Delta (\KE + \IE + \PE) = Q + W,
\ee
where the total energy $E$ is the sum of the kinetic energy $\KE$, 
the internal energy $\IE$, and the potential energy $\PE$.  The
change in total energy is equal to the net energy $Q$ added to the system
as heat plus the net work $W$ done {\bf on} the system.

Next, we introduce
\begin{itemize}
\item $d(\cdot)$ as the {\bf exact differential}, which does not depend on the 
path taken through space of the thermodynamic parameters and depends only
on the initial states, and
\item $\delta(\cdot)$ as the {\bf inexact differential}, which depends on the
path taken through space of thermodynamic parameters.
\end{itemize}
The balance law then states
\be
dE = \delta Q + \delta W.
\ee


\subsection{Second Law of Thermodynamics}
The Second Law states that the infinitesimal increment of entropy $dS$ 
of a system from an infinitesimal transfer of heat $\delta Q$, divided
by the common temperature of the system and the surrounding that supplies the
heat, satisfies
\be
dS \geq \dst\frac{\delta Q}{T}.
\ee
For a reversible process, 
\be
dS = \dst\frac{\delta Q}{T}.
\ee
For an irreversible process, 
\be
dS > \dst\frac{\delta Q}{T}.
\ee

\subsection{First and Second Laws Combined}
For a reversible process, the first and second laws can be combined as
\be
\boxed{dE = T dS + \delta W, \hspace{1.0cm}\mbox{\bf 
(1st and 2nd Law, Reversible Form)}.}
\ee

\subsection{Maxwell Relations}
The Maxwell relations are a statement of equality between
{\em first} derivative of a function because of a quality possessed by 
the {\em second} derivative of that function.  Specifically, 
the Maxwell relations equate {\em first} derivatives of the thermodynamic
potential functions $E, F, G,$ and $H$, 
because the {\em second} derivates of those
same functions does not depend on the order of differentiation
with respect to the thermodynamic variables $P, V, T,$ and $S$.

The Maxwell relations make use of the {\bf Schwarz theorem}, which 
states that if function $f : \real^2 \mapsto \real$ has continuous 
second partial derivatives at a given point $\vp \in \real^2 = 
\left\{p_1, p_2\right\}^T$,\footnote{This relationship can be 
extended to general dimensionality of 
$f : \real^n \mapsto \real$, but this higher dimensionality is not used
in the development that follows.}
then 
\be
 \dst\frac{\partial^2 f(\vp)}{\partial x_i \; \partial x_k} = 
 \dst\frac{\partial^2 f(\vp)}{\partial x_k \; \partial x_i}
 \hspace{1.0cm}\mbox{\bf (Commutative)}
\ee
The Schwarz theorem establishes that the result of differentiation
of a function of two variables is independent of the order of 
that differentiation.

With this context in mind, consider now the {\em first} derivatives 
of the thermodynamic potentials with respect to their natural variables.
Specifically, we can write the four thermodynamic potentials as functions
of their natural variables:
\begin{align}
 E &= E(S, V), \\
 F &= F(V, T), \\
 G &= G(T, P), \\
 H &= H(P, S). 
\end{align}
Next, we take {\em first} derivatives of these potentials:
\begin{align}
\left( \frac{\partial E}{\partial S} \right)_V = T, & \hspace{1.5cm}
\left( \frac{\partial E}{\partial V} \right)_S = -P, \\ 
& \nonumber \\ 
%
\left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial V} \right)_T = -P, & \hspace{1.5cm}
\left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial T} \right)_V = -S, \\
& \nonumber \\
%
\left( \frac{\partial G}{\partial T} \right)_P = -S, & \hspace{1.5cm}
\left( \frac{\partial G}{\partial P} \right)_T = V, \\ 
& \nonumber \\ 
%
\left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial P} \right)_S = V, & \hspace{1.5cm}
\left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial S} \right)_P = T. 
\end{align}
Finally, we take {\em second} derivatives of the potential functions
with respect to the natural variables, which
allows for the {\em first} derivatives of the natural variables with 
respect to the natural variables themselves to be equated, as follows:
\be
 \frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial V \; \partial S} \implies 
 \left(\frac{  \partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S = 
 \left(\frac{- \partial P}{\partial S}\right)_V
\ee
\be
 \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial T \; \partial V} \implies 
 \left(\frac{- \partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V = 
 \left(\frac{- \partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T
\ee
\be
 \frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial P \; \partial T} \implies 
 \left(\frac{- \partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = 
 \left(\frac{  \partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P
\ee
\be
 \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial S \; \partial P} \implies 
 \left(\frac{  \partial V}{\partial S}\right)_P = 
 \left(\frac{  \partial T}{\partial P}\right)_S
\ee
The foregoing equations are useful for eliminating entropy $S$, which 
is difficult to measure in a laboratory setting, in favor of the 
other natural variables pressure $P$, volume $V$, and temperature $T$, 
all of which are relatively easier to measure.

Assume at time $t=(t_0 + \Delta t$), the piston moves left-to-right
along the $x$-axis with speed $u_P$, measured in the fixed 
laboratory reference frame.  
Assume the piston speed $u_P$ causes a shock to propagate through
the material with speed $U_S$.  We require the following:
\begin{align}
  0 < u_P &< +\infty \label{eq:up} \\
  0 < U_S &< +\infty \label{eq:us} \\
  u_P &< U_S \label{eq:upltus}
\end{align}
From these considerations, we conclude that the material to the left of the
shock wave in Fig.~\ref{fig:piston_shock_cv} moves to the right at speed
$u_p$.  The material to the right of the shock wave remains at 
rest.\footnote{Furthermore, we assume the material throughout the tube and
for all time is inviscid, non-diffusive, and non-reacting.  We assume 
an adiabatic boundary, thus no
heat conduction occurs through the boundary of the tube walls or piston.
We assume no heat transfer from radiation occurs across the shock.  Finally, 
we assume body forces from gravity and electromagnetism are negligible.}

Since the piston compresses the material in the tube, we know
\begin{itemize}
\item $\rho_1 > \rho_0$,
\end{itemize}
where $\rho_1$ is the density of the material to the left of the shock.

Allow some time interval $\Delta t$ to elapse, as illustrated 
in Fig.~\ref{fig:piston_shock_cv}.  Consider the distances along the
$x$-axis, written as a multiplication of speeds and elapsed time.  
In the time interval $\Delta t$, the piston moves along the $x$-axis
a distance of $u_P \cdot \Delta t$.  
In the same time interval, the shock wave moves along the $x$-axis
a distance of $U_S \cdot \Delta t$.  Thus, the control volume at 
$t=(t_0 + \Delta t)$ has an axial length of $(U_S - u_P) \cdot \Delta t$.

From these axial lengths and piston cross-sectional area $A$, we can write
the following relationships:

\begin{itemize}
\item volume
\begin{itemize}
%
\item[] $V_0 = \left.V\right|_{t=t_0} = A \cdot U_S \cdot \Delta t$,
\item[] $V_1 = \left.V\right|_{t=(t_0 + \Delta t)} = A \cdot \left(
U_S - u_P\right) \cdot \Delta t$,
\end{itemize}
%
\item density
\begin{itemize}
\item[] $\rho_0 = \left.\rho\right|_{t=t_0}$,
\item[] $\rho_1 = \left.\rho\right|_{t=(t_0 + \Delta t)}$,
\end{itemize}
%
\item mass 
\begin{itemize}
\item[] $\rho_0 V_0 = \rho_0 \cdot A \cdot U_S \cdot \Delta t$, 
\item[] $\rho_1 V_1 = \rho_1 \cdot A \cdot \left(U_S - u_P\right) \cdot 
\Delta t$.
\end{itemize}
%
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Conservation of Mass}
Conservation of mass within the control volume requires that mass at
time $t=(t_0 + \Delta t)$ be unchanged from mass at time $t=t_0$.  
Thus
\be
 \rho_0 \cdot \cancel{A} \cdot U_S \cdot \cancel{\Delta t} =
 \rho_1 \cdot \cancel{A} \cdot \left(U_S - u_P\right) \cdot 
 \cancel{\Delta t}, 
\ee
or
\be
 \boxed{\rho_0 U_S = \rho_1 \left(U_S - u_P\right), 
 \hspace{1cm}
 \mbox{\bf{(Conservation of Mass)}.}}
\ee
Using specific volume (\ref{eq:specific_volume}), 
conservation of mass be rewritten as
\be
\svol_1 = \svol_0 \left( 1 - \dst\frac{u_P}{U_S} \right),
\ee
or
\be
\boxed{
\svol_1 - \svol_0 = \dst\frac{-v_0 \; u_P}{U_S},
\hspace{1cm}\mbox{\bf{(Conservation of Mass, Volume Form)}.}}
\label{eq:mass_conservation_alt}
\ee
From (\ref{eq:up})--(\ref{eq:upltus}), we know 
$\svol_1 < \svol_0$, which makes sense since we are compressing
the material and generally have the understanding that increased pressures
causes decreased volumes.

\subsubsection{Conservation of Momentum}
The net driving force from the piston on the material is $(P_1 - P_0) \cdot A$.
Conservation of momentum requires $F = d(ma)/dt$.  Written over the time
interval $\Delta t$ and for constant mass, 
conservation of momentum can be written
\be
 F \cdot \Delta t = m \cdot \Delta U.
\ee
Rewriting this expression in terms of pressures and densities, we have
\be(P_1 - P_0) \cdot \cancel{A} 
\cdot \cancel{\Delta t} = \left(\rho_0 \cdot \cancel{A} \cdot U_S \cdot 
\cancel{\Delta t} \right) \; \left(u_P - 0\right) 
\ee
which gives
\be
\boxed{P_1 - P_0  = \rho_0 \; U_S \; u_P,
\hspace{1.0cm}
\mbox{\bf{(Conservation of Momentum)}.}}
\label{eq:cons_momentum}
\ee
Using (\ref{eq:specific_volume}), (\ref{eq:cons_momentum}) can be
rewritten as
\be
\boxed{
P_1 - P_0 = \dst\frac{U_S \; u_P}{\svol_0},
\hspace{1.0cm}
\mbox{\bf{(Conservation of Momentum, Volume Form)}.}}
\label{eq:momentum_conservation_alt}
\ee

\subsubsection{Conservation of Energy}
The work done on the control volume by the piston moving left-to-right
is given by
\be
W_P = \underset{\mbox{\footnotesize force}}{\underbrace{P_1 \cdot A}}
\cdot 
 \underset{\mbox{\footnotesize disp.}}{\underbrace{u_P \cdot \Delta t}}.
\ee
Note that the right boundary of the control volume, located at the 
shock location, does not move, so the work across this boundary is 
identically zero.

The kinetic energy increase of the control volume is simply the one-half times
the mass times the square of the change in velocity, thus
\be
KE = \half  \;
\underset{\mbox{\footnotesize mass}}{\underbrace{\left(\rho_0 \cdot A 
\cdot U_S \cdot \Delta t\right)}} \;
\underset{\mbox{\footnotesize velocity}}{\underbrace{\left(u_P - 0
\right)}}^2.
\ee
Finally, the increase in internal energy is just the internal energy 
{\bf per unit mass}, $\senergy$, multiplied by the mass, 
\be
IE = (\senergy_1 - \senergy_0)  \;
\underset{\mbox{\footnotesize mass}}{\underbrace{\left(\rho_0
 \cdot A \cdot U_S \cdot \Delta t\right)}}.
\ee
Equating these quantities gives
\be
P_1 \cdot \cancel{A} \cdot u_P \cdot \cancel{\Delta t} = 
\half \; \rho_0 \cdot \cancel{A} \cdot U_S \cdot \cancel{\Delta t}
\cdot u_P^2 + 
\left(\senergy_1 - \senergy_0\right) \; \rho_0 
\cdot \cancel{A} \cdot U_S \cdot 
\cancel{\Delta t}
\ee
or simply
\be
\boxed{P_1 \; u_P = \rho_0 \; U_S \left(\frac{u_P^2}{2} + 
\senergy_1 - \senergy_0\right),
\hspace{1.0cm}
\mbox{\bf{(Conservation of Energy)}.}}
\label{eq:cons_energy}
\ee
The foregoing equation can be written without kinematic variables 
$U_S$ and $u_P$. 
Next, note that conservation of mass can be rewritten as 
\be
\frac{u_P}{U_S} = 1 - \frac{\rho_0}{\rho_1}.
\label{eq:cons_mass_ratio}
\ee
Then, with (\ref{eq:cons_energy}),
\begin{align}
P_1 \; u_P 
&\overset{(\ref{eq:cons_momentum})}{=} 
\left(P_1 - P_0\right) \frac{u_P}{2} + \rho_0 \; U_S \; 
(\senergy_1 - \senergy_0), \\
&= -P_0 \; u_P + 2 \; \rho_0 \; U_S \; (\senergy_1 - \senergy_0), \\
(\senergy_1 - \senergy_0) &= \frac{u_P \; (P_1 + P_0)}{2 \; \rho_0 \; U_S}, \\
&\overset{(\ref{eq:cons_mass_ratio})}{=} 
\left(\frac{P_1 + P_0}{2\;\rho_0}\right) \; 
\left(1 - \frac{\rho_0}{\rho_1}\right), \\
&\overset{(\ref{eq:specific_volume})}{=} 
\left(\frac{P_1 + P_0}{2/\svol_0}\right) 
\left(1 - \frac{\svol_1}{\svol_0}\right), 
\end{align}
which simplifies to
\be
\boxed{\senergy_1 - \senergy_0 = \frac{-1}{2}
\left(P_1 + P_0\right) \; (\svol_1 - \svol_0),
\hspace{1.0cm}
\mbox{\bf{(Rankine-Hugoniot curve)}.}}
\label{eq:hugoniot}
\ee
The Rankine-Hugoniot equation is the Conservation of Energy jump condition with
state variables alone; there are no kinematic variables in the equation.
To plot the Rankine-Hugoniot 
relationship in the $P\svol$ plane with $\senergy_1 > \senergy_0$, 
the initial state
$(\svol_0, P_0)$ must be known.

\subsubsection{Speed-Volume-Pressure Relationships}
The alternative forms for mass conservation (\ref{eq:mass_conservation_alt})
and momentum conservation (\ref{eq:momentum_conservation_alt}), 
when multiplied together or divided by one another, readily 
allow for the shock speed $U_S$ and particle speed $u_P$ to be written 
in terms of volume and pressure.  These relationship are found to be
\be
 U_S = \svol_0 \sqrt{\dst\frac{P_1 - P_0}{-(\svol_1 - \svol_0)}} 
     = \svol_0 \sqrt{\dst\frac{\Delta P}{-\Delta \svol}},
	 \label{eq:shock_PV}
\ee
and
\be
 u_P = \sqrt{- (P_1 - P_0) \; (\svol_1 - \svol_0)} = 
 \sqrt{- \Delta P \; \Delta \svol}.
 \label{eq:particle_PV}
\ee

These four thermodynamic states lie on a locus of all possible 
thermodynamic states that can exist in the material behind a shock.  This
locus is referred to as the {\bf shock Hugoniot}.  The curve connecting the
locus is the {\bf Rankine-Hugoniot curve}.  The line connecting two end
states is the {\bf Rayleigh line}.

It is important to emphasize that the Rankine-Hugoniot curve
{\bf is not} the path taken
by the material between two thermodynamic states.  

Note that (\ref{eq:shock_PV}) describes the mathematical increase in shock
speed with increasing slope 
of the Hugoniot, indicated in Fig.~\ref{fig:PV_diagram}.
Specifically, the slope of the Hugoniot goes as the negative squared value
of the shock speed $U_S$ per initial specific volume $\svol_0$, or
\be
\dst\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta \svol} = -\left(\frac{U_S}{\svol_0}\right)^2.
\ee

\section{Conservation of Anything}
%====================
Let the following definitions be made:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item $\real$ is a field of real numbers,
 \item $\bbEthree$ is three-dimensional Euclidean space,
 \item $V \subset \bbEthree$ is an arbitrary volume of space with 
 infinitesimal volume $dV$,
 \item $\partial V$ is the boundary of the volume $V$ with infinitesimal
 surface area $dA$,
 \item $d\ve{n} \in \realthree$ is the unit outward normal vector 
 of infinitesimal surface area $dA$ on boundary $\partial V$,
 \item $d\vA$ be the outward normal with magnitude $dA$ and direction 
 $d\ve{n}$,
 \item $\bbT = [t_0, t_f] \subset \real$ is the time interval of interest
 between initial time $t_0$ and final time $t_f$.  Note, $t_0$ is not 
 necessarily equal to zero, and the time interval may include negative 
 numbers, in addition to the commonly used positive numbers.  
\end{enumerate}

Let $f(\vx,t): V \times \bbT \mapsto \real$ 
be a function that describes a material property,
defined as item per unit of volume $V$, 
varies according to current position $\vx$ and time $t$.

There are two special cases:
\begin{itemize}
 \item $f = f(t)$ is referred to as {\bf uniform} since $f$ does not
 vary with position $\vx$.  
 \item $f = f(\vx)$ is referred to as {\bf constant} since $f$ does not 
 vary with time $t$.  
\end{itemize}

Two equivalent viewpoints are explicated: Eulerian and Lagrangian.  
With Eulerian, the volume $V$ is fixed in space for all time.  
With Lagrangian, the volume $V$ moves through space and time.  

\subsubsection{Eulerian formulation}
Let point $\vp(\vx) : V \mapsto \realthree$ 
be a point fixed in space for all time.  Point $\vp$ is 
located by Cartesian coordinates $(x,y,z)$, thus $\vp(\vx) = \vp(x,y,z)$.  
Note that $\vp$ is not a function of time $t$.
Let the vicinity of volume $V$ be located by point $\vp$.

How can property $\Phi$ be varied within the volume $V$?  It can be varied
either from {\em within} the volume or {\em across} the volume surface, 
referred to {\bf source} and {\bf flux}, respectively.
\begin{itemize}
 \item {\bf Source} (or more precisely, point source) says that at point $\vx$, 
 the property $\Phi$ is created 
 (added) or deleted (subtracted) from a point source or sink, respectively.  
 Let source $\dot{\Phi}_*(\vx,t)$ denote the property source/sink rate 
 at point $\vx$ and time $t$ with dimensions of 
 property per unit volume per unit time.  
 \item {\bf Flux} describes the property moving across the boundary 
 $\partial V$.  Movement can arise in two ways:
 \begin{enumerate}
  \item Absent of fluid transport:  Let $\vq$ be the flux of $\Phi$ in the 
  absence of fluid transport; for example, with heat conduction.  
  \item Presence of fluid transport: Let $\Phi \vv$ 
  be the transport flux of the property 
  because of fluid transport, with units of property
  per unit area per unit time.
 \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
Finally, we state that property $\Phi$ is {\it conserved} because changes in 
time of $\Phi$ across the volume $V$ are caused by either sources 
or fluxes.    
Stated mathematically, 
\be
 \frac{d}{dt} \int_V \Phi \; dV = 
 \underbrace{\int_V \dot{\Phi}_* dV}_{\mbox{\small source}} 
 \hspace{0.25cm} + \hspace{0.25cm} \underbrace{\left\{
	  \; - \int_{\partial V} \vq \cdot \vn \; dA  
	  \;\; - \;\; \int_{\partial V} \Phi \vv \cdot \vn \; dA \;
  \right\}}_{\mbox{\small flux}}
 \label{eq:conservation}
\ee
Note the minus signs for the flux terms are used with the understanding that 
movement of the property from inside to outside the volume 
(positive outward flux) {\em reduces} the time rate of change of the property.  

Next, recall the divergence theorem, also known as Gauss's theorem, 
relates flow (flux) of a vector field through a surface to the behavior 
of the vector field inside of the surface.  That is, for a volume $V$ that 
is compact and has a piecewise smooth boundary $\partial V$ and unit outward 
normal $\vn$, if $\vf$ is a continuously differentiable vector field defined 
in the vicinity of $V$, then 
\be
 \int_{\partial V} (\vf \cdot \vn) dA 
 = \int_V (\nabla_x \cdot \vf) dV  
\ee 
Use of the divergence theorem used for the flux terms in 
(\ref{eq:conservation}) gives
\be
 \frac{d}{dt} \int_V \Phi \; dV 
 = \underbrace{\int_V \dot{\Phi}_* dV}_{\mbox{\small source}} 
 \hspace{0.25cm} - \hspace{0.25cm} \underbrace{
	  \int_V \nabla_x \cdot \left(\vq + \Phi \vv \right) dV
  }_{\mbox{\small flux}}
 \label{eq:conservation_two}
\ee
Because the volume $V$ and surface $\partial V$ are fixed in 
an inertial reference frame, the time derivative of summed properties 
equals the sum of local time derivatives of properties, or
\be
 \frac{d}{dt} \int_V \Phi dV = \int_V \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial t} dV
\ee
Use of the foregoing equation in (\ref{eq:conservation_two}) gives
\be
 \int_V \left(
 \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial t} + \nabla_x 
 \cdot \left(\vq + \Phi \vv \right) \right) dV
 = 
\underbrace{\int_V \dot{\Phi}_* dV}_{\mbox{\small source}} 
\ee
Since the volume $V$ has arbitrary shape and size, the following must 
also be true, which is the general form for all conservation laws in 
continuum mechanics, 
\be
 \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial t} + \nabla_x \cdot 
 \left(\vq + \Phi \vv \right) = 
 \dot{\Phi}_*
 \label{eq:conservation_general}
\ee

\section{The Material Derivative}
%====================
Let $\vx = \vvarphi(\vX, t)$ be the function of a particle 
(of solid, fluid, or gas) at time $t$ that
was located at position $\vX$ at time $t=0$.  
Let some scalar property $\Phi$ (for example, temperature or density) of the 
fluid be attached the fluid at $\vX$ and as this point located by $\vX$ moves, 
the property $\Phi$ is invariant in time.  This means
\be
 \left.\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial t}\right|_{\vX} = 0.
 \label{eq:material_invariance}
\ee
When a property $\Phi$ is invariant in time for a particle $\vX$ of the fluid, 
the invariant scalar is {\em material}.  

The Eulerian point of view of this same property $\Phi$ proceeds by 
selecting a point $\vx$ that is fixed in space, and expressing the 
material invariance in terms of properties of the fluid {\em at} point $\vx$. 
In general, the fluid will be moving at this point $\vx$, so the velocity
$\vv$ of the fluid at this point enters through the chain rule.  
That is
\be 
 \left.\frac{\partial \Phi(\vvarphi(\vX,t),t)}{\partial t}\right|_{\vX}
 \overset{(\ref{eq:material_invariance})}{=} 0
 = \left.\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \varphi_i}\right|_{t}
   \left.\frac{\partial \varphi_i}{\partial t}\right|_{\vX} + 
   \left.\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial t}\right|_{\vvarphi} 
 = \nabla_x \Phi \cdot \vv + \Phi_{,t}.
\ee
This gives rise to the notation of {\bf material derivative}, written with 
capital $D$ as
\be
 \frac{D(\bullet)}{Dt} \defe \frac{\partial (\bullet)}{\partial t} + \vv \cdot
  \nabla_x(\bullet).
\ee

\section{Conservation of Mass}
%====================
Conservation of mass is obtained from (\ref{eq:conservation_general}) 
with the following substitutions:
\begin{itemize}
 \item $\Phi = \rho(\vx, t)$ is the mass per unit volume (or density),
 \item $\vq = \ve{0}$ because flux of mass can only occur with transport, and 
 \item $\dot{\Phi}_* = 0$ since mass can be neither created or destroyed.
\end{itemize}
This gives conservation of mass, also called the {\bf continuity equation}
\be
 \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla_x \cdot \left( \rho \vv \right) = 0.
\ee

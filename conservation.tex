\section{Conservation of Anything}
%====================
\begin{itemize}
 \item Let $\Box \subset \realthree$ be an arbitrary volume of space, 
 \item Let $\partial\Box = S \subset \realtwo$ be the surface enclosing the volume, 
 \item Let $d\ve{S} \in \realthree$ be the outward normal vector of a small patch 
  on surface $\partial\Box$, and
 \item Let $\bbT = [0, T] \subset \realplus$ be the time interval of interest.
\end{itemize}

Let $f(\vx,t): \Box \times \bbT \mapsto \realplus$ 
be a function that describes a material property,
defined as item per unit of volume $\Box$, 
varies according to current position $\vx$ and time $t$.

There are two special cases:
\begin{itemize}
 \item $f = f(t)$ is referred to as {\bf uniform} since $f$ does not
 vary with position $\vx$.  
 \item $f = f(\vx)$ is referred to as {\bf constant} since $f$ does not 
 vary with time $t$.  
\end{itemize}

Two equivalent viewpoints are explicated: Eulerian and Lagrangian.  With Eulerian, the
volume $\Box$ is fixed in space for all time.  With Lagrangian, the volume $\Box$ moves through
space and time.  

\subsubsection{Eulerian formulation}
Let point $\vp(\vx) : \Box \mapsto \realthree$ 
be a point fixed in space for all time.  Point $\vp$ is 
located by Cartesian coordinates $(x,y,z)$, thus $\vp(\vx) = \vp(x,y,z)$.  
Note that $\vp$ is not a function of time $t$.
Let the vicinity of volume $\Box$ be located by point $\vp$.

How can property $f$ be varied within the volume $\Box$?  It can be varied
either from {\em within} the volume or {\em across} the volume surface, referred to 
{\bf source} and {\bf flux}, respectively.
\begin{itemize}
 \item {\bf Source} (or more precisely, point source) says that at point $\vx$, 
 the property $f$ is created 
 (added) or deleted (subtracted) from a point source or sink, respectively.  Let source
 $\fdotstar(\vx,t)$ denote the property source/sink rate 
 at point $\vx$ and time $t$ with dimensions of 
 property per unit volume per unit time.  
 \item {\bf Flux} describes the property moving across the boundary $\partial\Box$.  
 Movement can arise in two ways:
 \begin{itemize}
  \item Absent of fluid transport:  Let $\vq$ be the flux of $f$ in the absence of
  fluid transport; for example, with heat conduction.  
  \item Presence of fluid transport: Let $f \vv$ be the transport flux of the property 
  because of fluid transport, with units of property
  per unit area per unit time.
 \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
Finally, we state that property $f$ is {\it conserved} because changes in 
time of $f$ across the volume $\Box$ are caused by either sources 
or fluxes.    Stated mathematically, 
\be
 \frac{d}{dt} \int_{\Box} f \; d\Box = \underbrace{\int_{\Box} \fdotstar d\Box}_{\mbox{source}} 
 \hspace{0.25cm} + \hspace{0.25cm} \underbrace{\left\{
	  - \int_{\partial\Box} \vq \cdot d\vS  
	  - \int_{\partial\Box} f \vv \cdot d\vS 
  \right\}}_{\mbox{flux}}
 \label{eq:conservation}
\ee
Note the minus signs for the flux terms are used with the understanding that movement of
the property from inside to outside the volume (positive outward flux) 
{\em reduces} the time rate of 
change of the property.  

Next, recall the divergence theorem, also known as Gauss's theorem, relates flow (flux)
of a vector field through a surface to the behavior of the vector field inside of the 
surface.  That is, for a volume $V \subset \realthree$ that is compact and has a 
piecewise smooth boundary $S$ and outward unit normal $\vn$, 
if $\vF$ is a continuously differentiable 
vector field defined in the vicinity of $V$, then 
\be
 \int_S (\vF \cdot \vn) dS 
 = \int_V (\nabla \cdot \vF) dV  
\ee 
Use of the divergence theorem used for the flux terms in (\ref{eq:conservation}) gives
\be
 \frac{d}{dt} \int_{\Box} f \; d\Box = \underbrace{\int_{\Box} \fdotstar d\Box}_{\mbox{source}} 
 \hspace{0.25cm} - \hspace{0.25cm} \underbrace{
	  \int_{\Box} \nabla \cdot \left(\vq + f \vv \right) d\Box 
  }_{\mbox{flux}}
 \label{eq:conservation_two}
\ee
Because the volume $\Box$ and surface $\partial \Box$ are fixed in an inertial reference
frame, the time derivative of summed properties equals the sum of local time derivatives
of properties, or
\be
 \frac{d}{dt} \int_{\Box} f d\Box = \int_{\Box} \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} d\Box
\ee
Use of the foregoing equation in (\ref{eq:conservation_two}) gives
\be
 \int_{\Box} \left(
 \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \left(\vq + f \vv \right) \right) d\Box
 = 
\underbrace{\int_{\Box} \fdotstar d\Box}_{\mbox{source}} 
\ee
Since the volume $\Box$ has arbitrary shape and size, the following must also be true, 
which is the general form for all conservation laws in continuum mechanics, 
\be
 \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \left(\vq + f \vv \right) = \fdotstar
 \label{eq:conservation_general}
\ee

\section{The Material Derivative}
%====================
Let $\vx = \vvarphi(\vX, t)$ be the function of a fluid particle at time $t$ that
was located at position $\vX$ at time $t=0$.  
Let some scalar property $\cP$ (for example, temperature or density) of the fluid be
attached the fluid at $\vX$ and as this point located by $\vX$ moves, the property
$\cP$ is invariant in time.  This means
\be
 \left.\frac{\partial \cP}{\partial t}\right|_{\vX} = 0.
 \label{eq:material_invariance}
\ee
When a property $\cP$ is invariant in time for a particle $\vX$ of the fluid, 
the invariant scalar is {\em material}.  

The Eulerian point of view of this same property $\cP$ proceeds by 
selecting a point $\vx$ that is fixed in space, and expressing the 
material invariance in terms of properties of the fluid {\em at} point $\vx$. 
In general, the fluid will be moving at this point $\vx$, so the velocity
$\vv$ of the fluid at this point enters through the chain rule.  
That is
\be 
 \left.\frac{\partial \cP(\vvarphi(\vX,t),t)}{\partial t}\right|_{\vX}
 \overset{(\ref{eq:material_invariance})}{=} 0
 = \left.\frac{\partial \cP}{\partial \varphi_i}\right|_{t}
   \left.\frac{\partial \varphi_i}{\partial t}\right|_{\vX} + 
   \left.\frac{\partial \cP}{\partial t}\right|_{\vvarphi} 
 = \nabla \cP \cdot \vv + \cP_t.
\ee
This gives rise to the notation of {\bf material derivative}, written with 
capital $D$ as
\be
 \frac{D(\cdot)}{Dt} \defe \frac{\partial (\cdot)}{\partial t} + \vv \cdot
  \nabla(\cdot).
\ee

\section{Conservation of Mass}
%====================
Conservation of mass is obtained from (\ref{eq:conservation_general}) with the following 
substitutions:
\begin{itemize}
 \item $f = \rho(\vx, t)$ is the mass per unit volume (or density),
 \item $\vq = \ve{0}$ because flux of mass can only occur with transport, and 
 \item $\fdotstar = 0$ since mass can be neither created or destroyed.
\end{itemize}
This gives conservation of mass, also called the {\bf continuity equation}
\be
 \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \left( \rho \vv \right) = 0.
\ee

\abstract{A phenomenological model of cavitation is presented, based on 
observations that both high relative negative pressure and high relative 
negative time gradients
of pressure are required for cavitation onset.  We simulated two cavitation 
experiments to generate cavitation scaling parameters for relative pressure 
drop and relative pressure drop rate.  The parameters were then used in a 
computational model to predict both the location of cavitation
and the probability of cavitation.  Our results show the model, while simple, 
is effective at reproducing results from laboratory experiments of cavitation.  
We used our verified model as a predictive tool for cavitation expectation in the 
human brain, secondary to blast exposure.  The results suggest
that the magnitudes of blast overpressure observed in field data is sufficient to cause 
intracranial cavitation.}

\section{Introduction}
Cavitation in a fluid, such as water, is the phenomenon of vapor-containing
bubble formation, caused by a rapid drop of pressure in the fluid.  
Conceptually, the pressure drop attempts to ``pull apart'' liquid water under
high tension, causing the water volume to expand rapidly.  The high pressure
drop leads to a water phase change, from liquid to vapor, and small vapor 
cavities (bubbles) appear throughout the liquid.  
    
Once formed, the vapor-filled bubbles are highly unstable, leading to a quick collapse, 
returning the vapor back to a liquid.  While the bubbles generally have spherical
symmetry, the collapse of the bubble generally is asymmetric, brought about 
by boundary conditions in the liquid, such as an adjacent hard surface or liquid
impurity.  The asymmetric collapse forms a water jet, with significant 
pressure elevation capable of eroding adjacent surfaces, such as the erosion
seen in propellers, impellers, and pumps.

The formation and collapse of cavitation bubbles has long been implicated
as a brain injury mechanism.  [Expand and cite].  

While existing models have sought to predict intracranial cavitation, 
they are complex in their formulation, and are prone to mesh-dependent errors 
caused by multi-scale effects.  We hypothesized that a simpler, phenomenological
model might be sufficiently endowed to capture cavitation events without 
suffering undue complexity brought on by multi-scale behaviors.  Moreover, to 
our knowledge, this is the first study that ties human head exposure of blast overpressure to
injury risk through expectation of cavitation mapped to human brain anatomy.

In the following sections, we motivate the need for a simplified, phenomenological
model.  We then explicate the model formulation and details.  Next, we apply
the model to a conceptual scenario, two cavitation experiments, and a human head
numerical surrogate.  We then summarize and discuss the results, followed by our
conclusions drawn from this modeling effort.

\subsection{Model Formulation}

The objective of the phenomenological model is to relate a mechanical state, i.e., pressure, that is favorable for a cavitation bubble to form with a probability.
These probabilities vary in time due to the dynamics of the system and are integrated in time to produce the final likelihood of cavitation as a spatial varaible.
In what follows, we denote the probability of an event ``C'' at a specific location and time as $\textrm{Pr}(C; X, t)$.
The total liklihood of an event ``C'' occuring at some location $X$ is then:
\begin{equation}
\textrm{Pr}(C;X) = \textrm{min}\left(1, \int_0^\infty \textrm{Pr}[C;X,t]\textrm dt\right)
\end{equation}
In the finite element model, $\textrm{Pr}(C;X)$ is a state variable that is integrated using a forward-Euler method where the instantaneous probability $\textrm{Pr}(C;X,t)$ is determined by the following criteria.
Pressure $P = P(X, t)$ is a time and space dependent variable, with positive 
pressures taken as compressive and negative pressures taken as tensile.
The first criteria is that for the probability of cavitation to be non-zero, the pressure, $P$, needs to be sufficiently tensile as to exceed a threshold for cavitation, $P_c$, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
 \mbox{\tt if } P < P_c \hspace{1cm} \mbox{ note: need bounds on } P_c
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \mbox{\tt then } \textrm{Pr}(C;X,t) = \alpha   > 0, \hspace{1cm} \mbox{ s.t. } \alpha > 0
\end{equation}
Alpha is the probability of cavitation occuring in a stable tensile state.
The second criteria that adds to the probability of cavitation is that if the pressure is rapidly decreasing, i.e., 
\begin{equation}
 \mbox{\tt and if } \displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} < 0
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \mbox{\tt and if } \displaystyle\abs{\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}} < \delta_h,  \hspace{1cm} \mbox{ s.t. } 0 < \delta_h < \infty
\end{equation}
(implied) the pressure frequency is below the high-frequency cutoff (disregard noise), 
\begin{equation}
 \mbox{\tt and if } \displaystyle\abs{\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}} > \delta_l,  \hspace{1cm} \mbox{ s.t. } 0 < \delta_l < \delta_h
\end{equation}
(implied) the pressure onset is sufficient to overcome inertia and surface tension, (disregard quasi-statics), 

%\begin{equation}
% \mbox{\tt then } E(c) = \alpha P + \beta \abs{\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}} > 0, \hspace{1cm} \mbox{ s.t. } \alpha > 0, \beta > 0,
%\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \mbox{\tt then instead } \textrm{Pr}(C;X,t) = \alpha  + \beta  > 0, \hspace{1cm} \mbox{ s.t. } \alpha > 0, \beta > 0,
\end{equation}
When neither of these conditions occur the probability of cavitation is zero, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
 \mbox{\tt else } \textrm{Pr}(C;X,t) = 0,
\end{equation}
the likelihood of cavitation is greater than zero, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are {\em cavitation parameters} to be used to reproduce experimental results;
